Getting your NSW contract for sale ready before you list

In NSW you can’t start marketing a home until a contract for sale has been prepared. Getting it ready early is one of the simplest ways to avoid a stalled launch. This guide covers what the contract is, who prepares it, the documents that go with it, and what you can gather now. It’s general information for Newcastle and Lake Macquarie homeowners, not legal advice. Your solicitor or conveyancer prepares the contract and advises on your property.

Why the contract has to come first

NSW rules say a residential property can’t be offered for sale until a contract for sale has been prepared. The agent has to keep the draft contract, prepared by a solicitor or licensed conveyancer, available for buyers to inspect. The Law Society of NSW notes that putting a house on the market without a proper contract is an offence and can lead to a fine.

In practice that means ordering the contract when you choose your agent, not after the photos are done. If the contract isn’t ready, the campaign can’t go live, however good the marketing is.

Who prepares it

A solicitor or a licensed conveyancer prepares the contract, orders the required certificates and searches, and attaches them. You’ll need one anyway: NSW settlements are completed electronically, and each party has to be represented by a solicitor or conveyancer who uses the eConveyancing platform. For help choosing one, see Choosing a conveyancer or solicitor to sell your home.

Documents that must be attached

The required documents are set out in NSW conveyancing law (Schedule 1 of the Conveyancing (Sale of Land) Regulation 2022). For a typical house on Torrens title they include:

  • Title search (property certificate) showing who owns the land and what is registered on the title.
  • The registered plan for the lot.
  • Dealings on the title, such as easements, rights of way, restrictions on use and positive covenants. See easements & rights of way and restrictive covenants & 88B instruments.
  • A drainage (sewer) diagram showing sewer lines on the property. In most of the lower Hunter this comes from Hunter Water. Your solicitor or conveyancer usually orders it.
  • A planning certificate (section 10.7) from your council, such as the City of Newcastle or Lake Macquarie City Council. See NSW planning certificates.
  • A notice about smoke alarms and loose-fill asbestos insulation, unless it is already printed in the contract.
  • The prescribed cooling-off statement explaining the buyer’s cooling-off rights. See cooling-off & exchange of contracts.

Strata units, townhouses in community schemes and off-the-plan sales need extra documents, such as the strata plan and by-laws. If you’re selling a unit, also read selling apartments & units and strata special levies & building defects.

Why it matters: if a required document is missing, the buyer may be able to rescind (cancel) the contract within 14 days after exchange, unless settlement has already happened. That’s a good reason to let a professional assemble the contract rather than rushing it.

Other documents that often go with the contract

Depending on the property, your solicitor or conveyancer may also include or ask about:

  • Swimming pool or spa: a certificate of compliance, a certificate of non-compliance, or a relevant occupation certificate. See pool compliance certificates.
  • Land tax: a certificate showing whether land tax is owing.
  • Recent building work: approvals and certificates for renovations, extensions, granny flats or decks, and home warranty insurance where it applies. See unapproved works & building certificates and owner-builder work & home warranty insurance.
  • A survey if boundaries, fences or structures near the boundary are in question.
  • Inclusions and exclusions: what stays and what goes, such as light fittings, blinds, a dishwasher or a garden shed. See inclusions, fixtures & chattels.

Promises you make by selling

NSW law builds certain promises (warranties) from the seller into the contract unless the contract discloses otherwise. Examples the Law Society of NSW gives: that the land isn’t affected by certain government proposals, and that there’s no sewer on the land that isn’t shown on the drainage diagram. That’s another reason to tell your solicitor or conveyancer early about anything unusual, such as works done without approval, shared driveways or past flooding. See also flood & bushfire disclosure and mine subsidence awareness.

What you can gather now

  • Your full name(s) as shown on the title, and the property address and lot/plan number if you have them (your rates notice usually shows these)
  • Your latest council rates and water notices
  • Your mortgage lender and loan details, so the discharge can be planned (see mortgage discharge for sellers)
  • Any approvals, certificates or warranty papers for building work done while you’ve owned the home
  • The pool certificate, if you have a pool or spa
  • Strata or community scheme details (managing agent contact, levy notices), if applicable
  • The current lease and bond details if the property is tenanted (see selling with tenants)
  • Any agreements for leased items such as solar systems, hot water units or security equipment (see solar panels & batteries)
  • A short list of anything you think a buyer should know

Avoiding delays in Newcastle & Lake Macquarie

  • Order early. Certificates and searches come from different bodies (NSW Land Registry Services, your council, Hunter Water). Ask your solicitor or conveyancer how long they expect it to take for your property.
  • Raise problems first. Unapproved works, boundary issues or a non-compliant pool are easier to deal with before buyers see the contract.
  • Agree on the settlement period and special conditions with your solicitor or conveyancer before listing, so your agent can answer buyers’ questions. See settlement period & vendor terms.
  • Keep your agent in the loop. Your agent can’t give legal advice, but they need to know when the contract will be ready to plan the launch.

FAQ

Can I start advertising my home before the contract for sale is ready?

No. In NSW a residential property can’t be offered for sale until a contract for sale has been prepared, and the agent must have the draft contract available for buyers to inspect. Order the contract when you choose your agent so it doesn’t hold up the launch.

Who prepares the contract for sale in NSW?

A solicitor or a licensed conveyancer. They order the title search, plan, drainage diagram, council planning certificate and other required documents and attach them to the contract.

What happens if a required document is missing from the contract?

If a prescribed document isn’t attached, the buyer may be able to rescind the contract within 14 days after exchange, unless settlement has already happened. Your solicitor or conveyancer will check the contract is complete.

Do I need a pool certificate to sell in NSW?

If the property has a swimming pool or spa, the contract generally needs a certificate of compliance, a certificate of non-compliance or a relevant occupation certificate. Ask your solicitor or conveyancer which applies to your pool.

Is a strata unit contract different?

Yes. Strata and community scheme lots need extra documents, such as the strata plan and by-laws. Your solicitor or conveyancer will know what your scheme requires.
