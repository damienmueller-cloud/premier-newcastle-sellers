How to read a comparative market analysis (CMA) from your agent

When you ask an agent for an appraisal, you’ll often get a comparative market analysis, or CMA. It’s the report that explains how they arrived at a likely selling range for your home. This guide shows Newcastle and Lake Macquarie sellers how to read one, what to check and what to ask. It builds on Understanding comparable sales in the Hunter and What is my house worth in Newcastle.

What a CMA is, and what it isn’t

A CMA compares your home with similar properties that have recently sold, and often with homes currently for sale, to support the agent’s estimate of what your home is likely to sell for. It’s an agent’s professional opinion based on market evidence.

It isn’t a formal valuation. If you need a valuation for a lender, a family law matter or an estate, that’s usually done by a qualified property valuer, which is a separate service.

Why it matters in NSW

When you sign an agency agreement in NSW, the agent must include an estimated selling price. NSW Fair Trading says that estimate must be reasonable, based on things like recent comparable sales, location, property features and current market conditions, and the agent must give you evidence to support it. If market conditions change, the agent must tell you in writing, give you new evidence and amend the agreement. A CMA is often the evidence behind that estimate. It also links to the rules against underquoting: see NSW price guides & underquoting.

What’s usually in a CMA

  • Your property summary: land size, bedrooms, bathrooms, parking, condition and notable features.
  • Recent sold comparables: address, sale date, sale price and key features of each, ideally with notes on how each compares to your home.
  • Current listings: homes you’d be competing with. These show competition, not proven value.
  • Unsold or withdrawn listings (sometimes): useful signs of where buyers have pushed back.
  • Market commentary: the agent’s view of buyer demand for your type of home.
  • The estimate and a recommendation: a likely selling range, and often a suggested sale method and marketing approach.

How to judge the sold comparables

The sold evidence is the heart of the report. For each comparable, ask:

  • Is it recent? Older sales may not reflect today’s buyers. Check the sale date, not just the price.
  • Is it really similar? Look at property type, land size, bedrooms and bathrooms, condition and renovation level, parking, aspect and outlook.
  • Is the location truly comparable? Two homes in the same suburb can sit in different markets. A main road, a flood-affected street, a corner block or being on a busy rail line can all change value.
  • Are the differences explained? A good CMA says why your home should sell above or below each comparable, not just lists prices.
  • Was it a normal sale? Sales between family members or with unusual terms may not reflect the open market.

A good CMA draws on the most relevant sales in your area, whichever agency sold them. If you’re unsure how the comparables were chosen, ask.

Local factors in Newcastle & Lake Macquarie

Some features can make two nearby homes hard to compare. Expect your agent to account for them:

  • Lake Macquarie: waterfront, water views and lake access versus homes a few streets back. See Selling in Lake Macquarie.
  • Newcastle: character and heritage homes versus newer builds, and walkability to beaches, the harbour or the city. See Selling in Newcastle and heritage & character homes.
  • Planning and land factors: flood or bushfire planning controls, mine subsidence districts, easements and zoning. See flood & bushfire disclosure and mine subsidence awareness.
  • Units and townhouses: strata levies, building condition and parking can matter as much as floor size. See selling apartments & units.

Comparing CMAs from more than one agent

  • Put the reports side by side and see which sales appear in more than one. Shared comparables are a good sign the evidence is sound.
  • Where estimates differ, ask each agent to explain the difference using the evidence.
  • NSW Fair Trading advises sellers to be cautious of estimates that are much higher than others, as they may not be reasonable. The highest number isn’t automatically the best agent.
  • Look beyond the price: the method, marketing plan and how clearly the agent explained the evidence also matter. See How to choose a selling agent.

Questions to ask about your CMA

  • Which three sales are you relying on most for my home, and why?
  • What would put my home at the top of the range, and what would pull it down?
  • Which current listings will buyers compare mine with?
  • Have any similar homes failed to sell recently? What happened?
  • How does the sale method you recommend affect the price conversation?
  • How will you update the estimate if buyer feedback or the market changes during the campaign?

FAQ

What is a CMA in real estate?

A comparative market analysis (CMA) is a report an agent prepares comparing your home with similar homes that have recently sold, and often with homes currently for sale, to support their estimate of your likely selling price.

Is a CMA the same as a valuation?

No. A CMA is an agent’s opinion based on market evidence. A formal valuation is usually prepared by a qualified property valuer, for example for a lender or a legal matter.

Does a NSW agent have to give me evidence for their price estimate?

Yes. NSW Fair Trading says an agent must include a reasonable estimated selling price in the agency agreement and give the seller evidence to support it, such as comparable sales. If the market changes, they must update the estimate in writing with new evidence.

Why do current listings appear in a CMA?

Current listings show the homes buyers will compare yours with. They show competition, but asking prices aren’t proof of value. Sold prices are the stronger evidence.

What if two agents give me very different estimates?

Ask each to explain their estimate using the sales evidence. NSW Fair Trading advises caution with estimates much higher than others, as they may not be reasonable.
